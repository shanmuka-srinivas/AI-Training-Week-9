# One Hundred Percent Wholegrain Rye Sourdough

- recipe_id: R-102
- cuisine: german
- dietary_tags: vegan, dairy-free, contains-gluten
- yield: one 1.9 kg pan loaf
- hydration: 90%

## About this loaf

Almost everything a wheat baker knows is wrong here, and the sooner you accept
that the better the loaf will be. Rye carries pentosans rather than a workable
gluten network, so the mass in your bowl behaves like a stiff batter and not
like a dough. It will never pass a windowpane test. It will never hold a shape
on the bench. Trying to knead it only makes it stickier and more miserable, and
the bakers who fight it hardest get the worst loaves.

The acidity of the rye sour is doing structural work, not just flavour work. Rye
starch gelatinises at a lower temperature than wheat starch, and without enough
acid the enzymes in the flour break the starch down faster than the bake can set
it, which is how you end up with a gummy stripe through the middle of a
technically well-baked loaf. That is why the starter here runs at thirty percent
rather than the ten or fifteen a wheat loaf would take.

The third column is baker's percentage against the total rye flour weight. Note
that the water sits at ninety percent, which is very high by wheat standards and
completely ordinary by rye ones.

## Ingredients

| Ingredient | Weight | Baker's % |
|---|---|---|
| Wholegrain rye flour | 900 g | 100% |
| Water (32 C) | 810 g | 90% |
| Rye sour starter | 270 g | 30% |
| Fine sea salt | 18 g | 2% |
| Toasted caraway seed | 6 g | 0.7% |
| Total dough | 2004 g | 222.7% |

## Method

Toast the caraway in a dry pan over a low heat until it smells of aniseed and
smoke, about ninety seconds, then tip it straight out of the pan so it stops
cooking. Caraway left in a hot pan turns bitter in seconds.

Beat the rye sour starter into the warm water until the mixture is uniform and
slightly frothy. Warm water matters more here than in a wheat loaf because the
batter has no elasticity to trap warmth and the proof is short.

Add the flour, the salt and the caraway all at once and mix with a sturdy
spatula for four minutes. There is no autolyse step and no reason for one, since
there is no gluten network to develop. Mix until you can no longer see dry flour
and then stop. Every extra minute past that point simply degrades the starch.

Scrape the batter straight into a greased pan and smooth the top with a wet
spoon. There is no bulk ferment and no shaping stage. Wet the spoon again
whenever the batter starts to cling, and take a moment to press the batter into
the corners of the pan, because rye will not spread to fill them on its own.

Proof in the pan for three hours at 28 C, or until fine cracks open across the
smoothed surface. The cracks are the signal, not the clock. A rye that goes into
the oven before they appear will be dense; one that goes in long after they
appear will sink in the middle.

Bake at 250 C for fifteen minutes, drop the oven to 190 C, and continue for a
further seventy-five minutes. The loaf wants a long, low bake to set the starch
all the way to the centre.

## Storage and troubleshooting

Wrap the cooled loaf in linen and wait a full day before slicing. This is not
optional and it is not a tradition. The starch is still setting for many hours
after the loaf leaves the oven, and a rye cut on the day of baking will always
be gummy no matter how well you baked it.

Wrapped in linen and kept in a bread crock, the loaf improves for three days and
keeps for a fortnight. If it develops a chalky white bloom it has dried out, not
spoiled, and it makes excellent breadcrumbs.

A sunken top means the proof ran too long. A gummy centre with a good crust
means either the bake was cut short or you sliced it too early. A sour edge that
tastes acetic rather than lactic means the starter was cold when it went in.

## Allergen note

Contains rye gluten. Free from dairy, egg, nuts, fish and shellfish.
