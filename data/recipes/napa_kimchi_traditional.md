# Traditional Napa Cabbage Kimchi

- recipe_id: R-104
- cuisine: korean
- dietary_tags: gluten-free, dairy-free, contains-fish, contains-shellfish
- yield: 2.4 kg
- hydration: not applicable

## About this ferment

This is baechu kimchi in its ordinary winter form, the version a household would
make several kilograms of at a time rather than the quick cucumber ferments that
get made and eaten in a week. It is a lactic ferment: the salt suppresses the
organisms you do not want, the lactobacilli already living on the cabbage
survive the salt, and over the following weeks they turn the sugars in the
vegetables into lactic acid. Nothing is added to start it. The culture is
already on the leaves.

Two ingredients here carry allergens and they are the two that give the ferment
its savoury depth, so read the allergen note before you cook for anybody else.
The vegan card in this chapter is not a substitution list bolted onto this
recipe; it is a separate formula with its own weights, because taking the fish
sauce out changes the salt balance and the timing.

The third column is a percentage of the cabbage weight rather than a true
baker's percentage, because there is no flour here to measure against. It is
written the same way so the two halves of the chapter can be read together, and
because the brining salt is the number you will actually want to scale.

## Method

Split the cabbages lengthways into quarters, leaving the core attached so the
leaves stay joined at the base. Work the coarse sea salt between every leaf,
paying most attention to the thick white ribs near the core, which need roughly
twice as much as the thin green tips.

Lay the salted quarters cut side up in a large tub, weight them down, and leave
them for four hours, turning the quarters once halfway through. They are ready
when a thick rib bends double without snapping. Rinse three times in cold water
and drain upside down for an hour. Under-rinsed cabbage produces kimchi that is
simply salty; badly drained cabbage produces kimchi that goes slack and watery
in the second week.

Cook the glutinous rice flour with water into a thin porridge and cool it to
room temperature before anything else goes near it. The porridge feeds the
lactobacilli in the first days and helps the paste cling to the leaves. Beat in
the gochugaru, garlic, ginger, fish sauce and salted shrimp, then fold through
the radish and spring onion.

Rub the paste into the drained cabbage leaf by leaf, wearing gloves. Pack the
quarters into jars, pressing out every air pocket as you go, and leave a
generous gap at the top because the ferment will push liquid upwards. Fold the
outermost leaf of each quarter over the top as a cap.

Leave the sealed jars at 20 C for 2 days until the brine turns cloudy and tastes
faintly sour, then move them to 4 C and mature for three weeks before eating.
Open the jars over a sink on the second day, because the pressure that has built
up will otherwise put brine on your ceiling.

## Storage and troubleshooting

Kimchi keeps for six months in the fridge and continues to acidify the whole
time, slowly. It does not spoil so much as it becomes a different ingredient:
young kimchi is for eating raw, and month-four kimchi is for stews and pancakes
where its sourness is the point.

Keep everything under the brine. Anything that sits proud of the liquid will go
soft and grow a white film. That film, if it is flat and matt, is a harmless
yeast and can be lifted off. Anything fuzzy, raised, or coloured means the jar
goes in the bin.

If the ferment tastes flat rather than sour after the room temperature stage,
the kitchen was too cold and it needs another day. If it is fizzy and sharp
after one day, the kitchen was too warm and it should go straight into the
fridge.

## Allergen note

Contains fish from the anchovy fish sauce and crustacean shellfish from the
salted shrimp. Free from gluten, dairy, egg and nuts.
